# The Latest Keeper — Story Document

*This is a living design document for the narrative side of the project. Nothing here is final — it's the story as currently understood, meant to evolve as the game is built. Mechanical/engineering details live in the main `README.md`; this file is purely narrative.*

## Premise

The graveyard sits between two communities with irreconcilable beliefs about the dead:

- **The Village** believes every person — regardless of who they were in life — deserves to be honored in death. Burial is a right, not a reward.
- **The Cult** believes the dead should be given over to their leader — burned, offered up — as devotion to something greater than the individual. To them, the Village's insistence on honoring everyone equally is a kind of denial, even disrespect, of what death is actually for.

These two beliefs didn't always exist in opposition. They used to be one — now factured in two.

## The Leader

Long ago, a single leader held both beliefs — and both communities — together as one person. When that person's sense of self was torn apart, the unity broke with it, and the Village and the Cult split off as its two halves, each carrying one half of what was once whole.

**The player character is that leader.** They don't know it. Each "keeper" the player controls across the game is not a new, unrelated person taking over an inherited job — it is the same person, fractured across death and rebirth, slowly reassembling who they once were, one life at a time.

The keeper's body changes with each rebirth — sometimes male, sometimes female, never the same twice. What carries across lives isn't physical continuity; it's fragments of memory, surfacing unpredictably. This is deliberate: the player can't lean on "looking the same" to feel continuity. Whatever sense of "still the same person" exists has to come from somewhere else — which is part of the point. It reframes the "you're not special, just the current keeper" theme: the tragedy isn't that they're ordinary — it's that they believe they're ordinary while carrying, unrecognized, the one broken self whose reunification could heal the very split they keep unknowingly perpetuating through their own choice of burial rites.

## The Cycle: Death & Memory

- Death is not combat or failure — it comes through a **ritual cost**, a choice the keeper makes to end their own tenure, offering up what they didn't carry forward with them.
- Each death/rebirth returns a fragment of the leader's true memory — never all of it, never predictably. Most of who they were remains forgotten each time.
- The world's day count persists across deaths; each new keeper's personal story restarts at their own "Day 1."

## Mother Elsbet

Mother Elsbet is not just a Village-aligned NPC — she is the leader's spouse. She is also the one constant across every cycle: the keeper's body and self change life to life, but she does not.

She has always known. Every keeper who has cycled through since her spouse's fracturing has been them, and she has recognized it each time, even when they could not. But she does not intervene. She doesn't explain, doesn't correct, doesn't try to speed the process along — she stands by, present but not directive, and lets her spouse rebuild who they are from the ground up, on their own timeline, however long or painful that takes.

This is the emotional center of the story: not a mystery to be solved, but a portrait of what it looks like to love someone through the slow, non-linear work of rebuilding themself after being torn apart — without trying to do it for them, and without leaving.

**In dialogue, her "I've seen keepers before" is no longer wise-woman flavor text — it is literally true**, and everything she says to the player should eventually be re-readable, in hindsight, as a spouse speaking carefully and patiently to the person she loves, who doesn't yet know who she is to them.

## Why Neither Faction Turns Fully Against the Keeper

Reputation with the Village and Cult can rise and fall sharply based on burial choices, but neither faction is ever depicted as capable of truly turning on the keeper (no combat, no expulsion, no violence implied). Some instinctive, unconscious part of both communities still recognizes what the keeper actually is — the self they both split from — even though neither consciously remembers or understands why. Reputation swings represent real feeling and real consequence, but not an existential threat to the keeper's safety.

## The Sacrifice

Raising reputation with a given faction high enough unlocks that faction's sacrifice: a ritual, performed in the Cellar, where the keeper offers up part of who they are to that side of themself.

- The keeper is always given up when the ritual is performed — tenure ends, a new life begins.
- What's sacrificed *to the faction* is whatever the keeper chooses **not** to carry forward. What they do choose to carry — up to a handful of things — persists into the next life with them.
- This is not a loss condition. It's the mechanism of reunification: by reaching each half of themself in turn, and by being willing to let go of what they were in order to do it, the keeper moves closer to becoming whole — not the person they were before the schism, but the person they're choosing to become.

The intended shape of the ending: full reunification of Village and Cult, achieved by the keeper having reached and given themself to both halves across their lives. Not a reversal back to some prior wholeness, but a self rebuilt — deliberately, in pieces, with Elsbet having stood by for all of it without ever trying to make it happen faster than it could.

## The Villages

Scattered around the graveyard, eight villages sit in two mirrored hemispheres — four aligned with the Village, four with the Cult — each pair sharing a root that names one thing a keeper loses with every rebirth:

- **Grief** — Mournhollow (kept) / Ashmourn (burned)
- **Love** — Heartwarden (kept) / Heartforge (burned)
- **Guilt** — Penncross (kept) / Guiltpyre (burned)
- **Hope** — Hopewell (kept) / Hopeforsworn (burned)

A keeper doesn't start with access to all of them — each village is discovered as reputation with its faction grows, mirroring how a keeper only ever recovers fragments of who they were, never the whole picture at once.

## Design Intent Reminders

- Tone throughout should stay quiet, reflective, and matter-of-fact — even at the reveal. This is not a horror story or a triumphant chosen-one arc; the emotional register is closer to grief and quiet devotion than spectacle.
- The mystery should unfold the way the leader themself experiences it: partially, unreliably, without narration explaining things the player-character doesn't yet know.
- Elsbet's restraint is the throughline — she is defined by what she *doesn't* do as much as by what she does. Avoid writing her into moments where she rescues, corrects, or explains too much; her love is legible through presence and patience, not intervention.
- Nothing in this document should be treated as locked. As the mechanical game grows (more NPCs, the sacrifice/faction system, the Cellar's former-keeper records), this document should be revisited and revised to match what actually gets built.
