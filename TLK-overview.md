# The Latest Keeper — Premise & Story

*An overview of the game's premise, mechanics, and story, written for someone new to the project. It contains spoilers for the central twist.*

## The Pitch

*The Latest Keeper* is a graveyard management game. You run a graveyard that sits between two communities with opposite beliefs about the dead. Every day a body arrives, and what you do with it matters: how you prepare it, how you dispose of it, and which side you please. Underneath the daily routine is a story about a person who was torn in two, and who is slowly putting themself back together across many lives without knowing it.

The game is inspired by the flow and timing of *Graveyard Keeper*, but its rites are emergent rather than authored, its reputation system is built on tension between two factions, and its progress is a chain of lives instead of a single run.

There is no fixed burial menu. You gather reagents, combine them into a rite, and choose whether the body is buried or burned. The Village honors everyone, and the Cult gives the dead up to fire. Every choice pleases one side and costs you with the other, so no rite satisfies everyone, and the days never quite have enough hours for everything you'd like to do. Bodies rot if you leave them, villages need tending, and the people who live in them have small favors to ask.

Push far enough with one side and you unlock a ritual that ends your life and begins the next. You keep a few things, give up the rest, and wake as someone new, with only scattered fragments of who you were. Across eight lives and eight villages, you slowly learn what those fragments add up to.

The tone is quiet and unhurried. It's closer to grief and devotion than to horror, and the story trusts you to piece it together the way the keeper does: partially, and a little too late.

## The Setting

The graveyard sits between two communities:

- **The Village** believes every person, regardless of who they were in life, deserves to be honored in death. Burial is a right, not a reward.
- **The Cult** believes the dead should be given over to their leader, burned and offered up, as devotion to something greater than the individual. To them, the Village's insistence on honoring everyone equally is a kind of denial of what death is actually for.

These two beliefs did not always stand in opposition. They used to be one.

## The Central Twist

The Village and the Cult are the two halves of a single person.

Long ago, one leader held both beliefs, and both communities, together. When that person's sense of self was torn apart, the unity broke with it. The Village and the Cult split off as its two halves, destined to be forever at odds. Each carrying one half of what was once whole.

**The player character is that leader.** They do not know it. Each keeper the player controls is not a new, unrelated person inheriting an old job. It is the same person, fractured across death and rebirth, reassembling who they once were one life at a time.

The keeper's body changes with every rebirth: sometimes male, sometimes female, never the same twice. What carries across lives is not physical continuity but fragments of memory that surface unpredictably. This is deliberate. The player cannot lean on "looking the same" to feel continuity, so whatever sense of "still the same person" exists has to come from somewhere else.

It also reframes a familiar theme. The tragedy is not that the keeper is ordinary. It is that they believe they are ordinary while carrying, unrecognized, the one broken self whose reunification could heal the very split they keep unknowingly perpetuating through their choice of burial rites.

## A Day in the Graveyard

Time moves on a fixed clock. One in-game day is 7.5 minutes of real time, and one body is delivered per day. An unattended corpse decays, and one that reaches zero quality rots away. The core tension is always *what do I do, for whom, with the time I have today.*

Disposing of a body takes several steps:

1. **Autopsy.** Examine the corpse in the Morgue.
2. **Choose a method.** Dig a grave at the Burial Site with the Shovel, or light the Incinerator.
3. **Wait or work.** Digging is done by hand. The Incinerator burns on a timer, so the keeper can walk away and do other things while it does.
4. **Perform the rite.** Return with a chosen set of reagents and complete the disposal.

The rest of the day is filled with other work: gathering reagents, tending villages, and running errands for the people who live there.

## Rites and Reputation

There is no fixed burial menu. A **rite** is built from up to four **reagents**, and its effects come from the tags those reagents carry. There are twelve reagents: four base reagents (Salt, Prayer Candle, Grave Dirt, Iron Nail) that are cheap and plentiful, and eight village reagents that are scarce and only available by visiting and tending each village.

- **Reputation-linked tags** are `holy`, `profane`, `preserved`, and `bound`. The factions have opinions about these.
- **Theme tags** are `grief`, `love`, `guilt`, and `hope`. They carry no mechanical weight and exist for story.

Each tag's weight is the number of selected reagents carrying it, and each faction multiplies those weights by its own opinion of them. The Village approves of `holy` and dislikes `profane`. The Cult is the exact opposite. Stacking pushes one faction further, and mixing opposing tags cancels out.

The choice of disposal also matters. Burial adds a hidden `holy` weight, and burning adds a hidden `profane` one, so the method itself leans toward one side.

Each delivered corpse also asks for a specific tag, and a rite that includes it pays a bonus. The result is that one rite can raise one faction and lower the other, and no rite satisfies everyone. Every disposal is a decision.

## Burial and Burning

The two ways of disposing of a body are the two beliefs made physical:

- **The Burial Site** is the Village's belief. The body is honored, laid in the ground, and kept.
- **The Incinerator** is the Cult's belief. The body is given up to fire, and the keeper has to wait while it burns.

Every rite the keeper performs, whichever way, quietly leans one half of themself further than the other. The keeper never sees it as choosing between halves of who they are, only as the work of the day.

Occasionally a rite combines a reagent from the kept side and one from the burned side of the same theme, such as Mourning Bell Flower and Ember-Ash Bloom, both `grief`. This is called a **reconciled** rite. No faction reacts to it. It is the two halves briefly agreeing, and it is reserved for story moments that foreshadow the ending.

## The Cycle: Death and Memory

- **Death is not combat or failure.** It comes through a ritual cost, a choice the keeper makes to end their own tenure.
- **Each rebirth returns a fragment of the leader's true memory.** Never all of it, and never predictably. Most of who they were remains forgotten each time.
- **The world's day count persists.** Each new keeper's personal story restarts at their own Day 1, but the world has kept going.
- **Progress carries over in part.** Each new keeper begins with a fraction of the standing the previous one reached, which shortens the grind and reads as the self remembering just enough not to start from nothing. Discovered villages, story beats, memory fragments, and Cellar records all persist.

## The Sacrifice

Raising reputation with a faction high enough unlocks that faction's **sacrifice**, a ritual performed in the Cellar where the keeper offers up part of who they are to that side of themself.

- The keeper is always given up when the ritual is performed. Their tenure ends and a new life begins.
- What is sacrificed *to the faction* is whatever the keeper chooses **not** to carry forward. Up to five items can be carried into the next life.
- This is not a loss condition. It is the mechanism of reunification. By reaching each half of themself in turn, and by being willing to let go of what they were to do it, the keeper moves closer to becoming whole. Not the person they were before the split, but the person they are choosing to become.

## The Order of Lives

The full run is **eight lives**, played in Village/Cult pairs. Each pair shares a theme, and the keeper works through it in order:

- **The Village life comes first, its Cult mirror second.** The keeper reaches the kept half of a lost thing, then the burned half.
- **After each pair, the player chooses the next theme** from those not yet done. Narratively, the keeper is deciding which lost thing to recover next.
- **Later pairs demand more.** The reputation needed for each sacrifice rises with each pair, so the last lives are the longest.
- **There is no early ending.** Reaching one faction, or even one full pair, is not reunification. The run ends only after all eight sacrifices, because the leader has to reach every half of themself. The full run is about 44 hours, with the first life taking about 3.

## The Villages

Around the graveyard sit eight villages in two mirrored hemispheres, four aligned with the Village and four with the Cult. Each pair shares a root that names one thing a keeper loses with every rebirth.

| Theme | Kept (Village) | Burned (Cult) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Grief** | Mournhollow | Ashmourn |
| **Love** | Heartwarden | Heartforge |
| **Guilt** | Penncross | Guiltpyre |
| **Hope** | Hopewell | Hopeforsworn |

A keeper does not start with access to all of them. Each village is discovered as reputation with its faction grows, mirroring how the keeper only ever recovers fragments of who they were. Undiscovered villages appear as `???` on the map, with faint hints as you walk near them. Once discovered, each village is its own generated map with its own people, reagents, and daily tending.

## Mother Elsbet

Mother Elsbet is not just a Village-aligned NPC. She is the leader's spouse, and the one constant across every cycle. The keeper's body and self change from life to life, but she does not.

She has always known. Every keeper who has cycled through since her spouse's fracturing has been them, and she has recognized it each time, even when they could not. But she does not intervene. She does not explain, does not correct, and does not try to speed the process along. She stands by, present but not directive, and lets her spouse rebuild who they are from the ground up, on their own timeline, however long or painful that may be.

This is the emotional center of the story. It is not a mystery to be solved but a portrait of what it looks like to support someone through the slow, non-linear process of rebuilding after being torn apart, without doing it for them, and without hurrying them.

Her line "I've seen keepers like you before" is not wise-woman flavor text. It is literally true, and everything she says to the player should eventually be re-readable, in hindsight, as a spouse speaking carefully and patiently to the person she loves, who does not yet know who she is to them.

She also gives the keeper small tasks. These are errands and small kindnesses, never answers or hints. A quest from her asks for help with a small thing and tells the keeper nothing about who they are.

## The Cellar

The Cellar is where the sacrifice happens and where past keepers are recorded. Each record holds a name (Keeper #N), the span of days they served, an epitaph drawn from the memory fragment their sacrifice unlocked, and the faction and village they sacrificed to. If corpses rotted during their tenure, a short note records that they did not always return in time, and kept going anyway.

The records are the closest the keeper comes to seeing their own history, without being told what it means.

## Why Neither Faction Turns on the Keeper

Reputation with the Village and the Cult can swing sharply based on rite choices, but neither faction is ever capable of truly turning on the keeper. There is no combat, no expulsion, and no violence implied.

Some instinctive, unconscious part of both communities still recognizes what the keeper actually is, the self they both split from, even though neither consciously remembers or understands why. Reputation swings represent real feeling and real consequence, but never an existential threat.

## The Ending

The intended ending is full reunification of the Village and the Cult, achieved by the keeper having reached and given themself to both halves across their lives. It is not a reversal back to some prior wholeness. It is a self rebuilt deliberately, in pieces, with Elsbet having stood by for all of it without ever trying to make it happen faster than it could.

## Tone

Quiet, reflective, and matter-of-fact, even at the reveal. This is not a horror story or a triumphant chosen-one arc. The emotional register is closer to grief and quiet devotion than to spectacle. The mystery unfolds the way the leader experiences it: partially, unreliably, without narration explaining what the player-character does not yet know.

## Current Status

The game is an early playable prototype. Built so far: the corpse pipeline and daily clock, tag-based rites, two-faction reputation, village discovery and travel, tending, first-pass NPC quests, the sacrifice ritual, Cellar records, and save/load. Still to come: the reunification ending, the full content pass for the remaining villages, sprites and art, and the rest of the daily activities that fill each day.
